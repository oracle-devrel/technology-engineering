Tides are the slow breathing of the ocean, and for most of human history nobody knew what caused them. Sailors knew the rhythm well enough to plan a departure, and fishing villages built their days around the turn of the water, but the link to the Moon was a matter of folklore rather than physics. The clue was always there in the timing. High water arrives roughly fifty minutes later each day, and the Moon rises roughly fifty minutes later each day, so the two keep step like dancers who never quite look at each other.

The explanation that finally stuck is simple to state and surprisingly hard to picture. The Moon pulls on the near side of the Earth a little harder than on the centre, and on the centre a little harder than on the far side. The ocean, being free to move, stretches along that line of pull. The result is two bulges of water, one facing the Moon and one facing away, and the solid Earth turns underneath them once a day. A given harbour passes through both bulges, which is why most coasts see two high tides and two low tides in a little over twenty four hours.

The Sun plays the same game with less than half the strength. When the Sun and Moon line up, at new moon and full moon, their bulges add together and the range between high and low water is at its greatest. These are the spring tides, named for the way the water seems to leap, not for the season. A week later, when the Moon has moved a quarter of the way round its orbit, the two pulls work at right angles and partly cancel. The gentler neap tides are the result, and anyone who has tried to float a boat off a mud bank at the wrong time of the month has learned the difference the hard way.

Real coastlines complicate the picture. Water has to flow into bays and around headlands, and it sloshes in basins the way tea sloshes in a cup. In the open Pacific the tidal range is less than a metre, while at the head of the Bay of Fundy the funnel shape of the shore drives the range past fifteen metres. Some seas, like the Mediterranean, barely notice the Moon at all because their basins are too small and too enclosed for a bulge to build. Others have points where the tide hardly moves and the water simply rotates around them, a pattern that only became visible once satellite altimeters began mapping the sea surface from orbit.

The energy in all this motion has to come from somewhere. It comes from the spin of the Earth, which is slowing by about two milliseconds per century as the tides drag on the sea floor. The Moon takes the lost momentum and drifts outward by nearly four centimetres a year, a figure we know precisely because astronauts left mirrors on its surface and laser pulses have been bounced off them ever since. Hundreds of millions of years ago the day was shorter and the Moon loomed larger in the sky. Coral growth bands and layered sediments still record those faster rhythms, so the ocean has been keeping a diary of the Moon for as long as there has been an ocean.
